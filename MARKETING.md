# Marketing Strategy

## Status

Target go-to-market and growth strategy for the shopping-budget product.

The shipped product is Shopping Budget Companion. Marketing must distinguish implemented functionality from unvalidated performance, retention, PWA, and scanning claims.

This document is designed for an indie/portfolio launch first, with a path toward real consumer growth if the product proves retention.

It complements:

- BRAND.md — positioning, voice, identity
- PRODUCT.md — core product promise
- DESIGN.md — visual presentation
- docs/marketing/RESEARCH.md — evidence base

## Marketing objective

Do not optimize first for downloads.

Optimize for:

> people who immediately understand the problem, complete a first shopping trip, and return for another one

The product only becomes commercially interesting if repeated grocery/shopping use is real.

## Primary marketing thesis

The problem is not “people need another budget app.”

The problem is:

> **The moment you learn you exceeded your shopping budget is usually too late.**

Most financial tools explain spending after payment.

Our product gives feedback while the user can still remove or swap something.

That timing difference is the heart of all marketing.

## Core message

Primary:

> **Know what you can still afford before checkout.**

Functional:

Trust:

## Audience hierarchy

### Segment 1 — hard-limit grocery shoppers

Priority: **100/100**

Characteristics:

- fixed trip budget
- fixed/limited income
- cash-envelope mindset
- anxious about checkout total
- wants practical control, not financial education

Message:

> Going in with €50? Know exactly what’s left before you reach the register.

### Segment 2 — students / young adults

Priority: **92/100**

Characteristics:

- strict weekly grocery cap
- highly price-sensitive
- comfortable with fast mobile tools
- likely to respond to simple social demos

Message:

> Set €35. Add prices as you shop. Stop guessing.

### Segment 3 — families / larger grocery trips

Priority: **91/100**

Characteristics:

- many items
- cognitive load
- distractions
- more opportunity for mental-math error

Message:

> The bigger the cart, the harder mental math gets. Keep one number visible: what’s left.

### Segment 4 — gift-card / cash-envelope shoppers

Priority: **85/100**

Message:

> Know how much of your fixed balance is still safe to spend.

### Segment 5 — general “shopping optimizer”

Priority: **65/100**

This audience is too broad for launch messaging.

Do not dilute the brand into “for every purchase everywhere” until the grocery use case is proven.

## North-star growth metric

For real product validation:

> **Second-trip rate**

Definition:

Percentage of users who complete/start another real shopping trip within a reasonable window after first use.

Why:

- closer to actual utility than installs
- more meaningful than account creation
- product has no mandatory account
- naturally maps to weekly grocery behavior

## Launch phases

## Phase A — portfolio/private beta

Goal:

validate comprehension and interaction.

Channels:

- GitHub
- personal network
- developer communities
- direct test users

Need:

- reliable HTTPS beta build usable on a real phone
- polished enough core UI to test the shopping task
- clear problem/value explanation
- feedback capture

PWA installability is not a prerequisite for retention validation. It follows the repeat-trip gate unless real beta evidence shows installability itself is blocking repeated use.

## Phase B — focused public beta

Goal:

validate repeated shopping before scanner/OCR breadth or meaningful paid acquisition.

Recruit:

- 20–50 real shoppers
- strict-budget users
- students/families

Where practical, segment directionally:

- Cohort A — manual-first
- Cohort B — manual + Repeat Trip / Recent Items / Price Memory
- Cohort C — manual + experimental scanner, only if the early benchmark was positive

The cohort is for directional learning, not statistical significance.

Ask after actual trips:

- Did you use it in-store?
- At what item did entry begin to feel tedious?
- Did it change a purchase decision?
- What did you stop entering because it was annoying?
- Did the total feel trustworthy?
- Would you use it next week?
- Did you actually use it on your next trip?
- Did you return for a third trip?
- If you used scanning, did it actually save time or reduce effort?

Observed second/third-trip behaviour matters more than stated intent.

## Phase C — store launch

Only after retention evidence is credible enough to justify distribution work.

A strong second-trip result without healthy third-trip behaviour is not enough to justify aggressive scaling.

Before store launch, prefer the sequence:

1. polished manual core
2. Repeat Trip / Recent Items / Price Memory
3. 20–50 real-store beta users
4. retention analysis and core-friction fixes
5. store packaging/listing

Only after:

- core Tier 0 scenarios stable
- second-trip behavior exists
- product screenshots are final
- no known data-loss bugs
- privacy/store metadata ready

## Risk register

### Risk 1 — category is crowded

Severity: high.

Mitigation:

remaining-first positioning and execution quality.

### Risk 2 — scanner steals message

Severity: medium.

Mitigation:

scanner is creative hook, not core promise.

### Risk 3 — users see “budget” and expect finance management

Severity: high.

Mitigation:

always pair budget language with shopping/cart/checkout context.

### Risk 4 — subscription distrust

Severity: medium/high.

Mitigation:

generous free core, transparent pricing.

### Risk 5 — marketing outpaces product reliability

Severity: critical.

Mitigation:

never launch aggressive acquisition before persistence/Tier 0 flows are stable.
